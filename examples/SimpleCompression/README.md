# SimpleCompression — damage in a layered coal specimen, and its ERT signature

A numerical uniaxial compression test on a layered roof/coal/floor specimen,
built to study **how a developing damage zone shows up in ERT imaging**. The
chain is

    damage model  ->  porosity  ->  conductivity (Revil)  ->  synthetic ERT/IP survey

This directory currently implements the first stage: the compression test that
produces the damage and porosity fields. The petrophysical and survey stages come
next.

## The specimen

Three stacked 75 mm x 75 mm blocks, compressed along `z`:

```
  z = 100 mm  +-----------------+
              | roof: sandstone |  30 mm    E0 = 30 GPa, UCS ~ 48 MPa
  z =  70 mm  +-----------------+
              |      coal       |  40 mm    E0 =  3 GPa, UCS ~ 15 MPa
  z =  30 mm  +-----------------+
              | floor: shale    |  30 mm    E0 = 20 GPa, UCS ~ 30 MPa
  z =   0 mm  +-----------------+
```

Only the middle block is coal; the roof and floor are the much stiffer clastic
country rock of the coal measures. The seam therefore takes most of the
deformation (about twice the global axial strain) and fails first.

The `UCS` figures above are *unconfined* strengths (roughly `gamma*sigma_t`) and
are not predictions for this specimen: because the seam is bonded between much
stiffer rock it is laterally confined, which raises its strength considerably.
The assembly peaks near 34 MPa rather than at the coal's unconfined 15 MPa.

42 electrodes sit on the two opposite faces `x = 0` (24, in 4 rows of 6) and
`x = 75` (18, in 3 rows of 6), as given in `stations.csv` — a cross-hole style
geometry looking through the specimen across the seam.

**Units are mm throughout.** `gmsh2fly.py` places the electrodes at the
coordinates in `stations.csv`, which are in mm, so the mesh must use the same
units. Moduli and strengths are in Pa and strains are dimensionless, so stresses
come out in Pa; the localization length is a length and is therefore in mm too.

## Workflow

```bash
export OMP_NUM_THREADS=4
export PYTHONPATH=$PYTHONPATH:<fingal>/bin

python3 mkGeo.py                                    # stations.csv -> sample.geo
gmsh -3 -optimize_netgen -o sample.msh sample.geo   # ~3 s, 73k tets
gmsh2fly.py sample.msh config                       # -> sample.fly
python3 runCompression.py                           # the compression test
```

`mkGeo.py` writes the three-layer box and embeds each electrode as a mesh point
in the face patch of the layer it belongs to (`Point{p} In Surface{s};`), so the
mesh carries a node exactly at every electrode. The *tagging* of those nodes
(`s101`, `s1`, ...) is done by `gmsh2fly.py`, which adds them as dirac points
from the same `stations.csv` — which is why both read the one file.

`specimen.py` is the single source of truth for the geometry, the material
properties and the boundary conditions, shared by the mesh generator and the run
script.

## Material model

`SmoothDamageModel` (`fingal/damage.py`), the implicit-gradient regularised
continuum damage model of Mondal, Olsen-Kettle & Gross (2020). Each layer has its
own stiffness, strength, ductility and porosity, passed as `Data` fields.

Loading is **dissipation-controlled (arc-length)** rather than
displacement-controlled, so the post-peak softening branch — where the damage
band actually forms — can be traversed instead of stalling. `--displacement`
switches to plain displacement control.

### Statistical heterogeneity is not optional here

`E0` and `sigma_t` are drawn from a spatially correlated **Weibull**
distribution (the usual statistical-damage / RFPA approach), with the shape
parameter `m` acting as a homogeneity index — coal being the most heterogeneous
layer. A correlated Gaussian field is generated by the spectral (FFT) method of
the bundled `specsim3d` and mapped to Weibull marginals by the probability
integral transform, so the spatial correlation survives the transform.

This was not added only for realism. With **uniform** properties the specimen
does not fail in the coal at all — an elastic probe of where the equivalent
strain first reaches the damage threshold gives:

| properties | onset load factor | damage nucleates at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| uniform | 0.145 | floor rock, free edge at the coal contact |
| Weibull | 0.038 | **coal interior** |

Uniform properties put nucleation in a bimaterial free-edge stress
concentration, which is a mesh-sensitive singularity and not the seam failure the
study is about — the coal needed 1.9x more load than that artefact. With Weibull
strength, nucleation moves into the bulk of the seam and the coal leads the floor
(0.038 vs 0.052).

Three consequences worth knowing:

- `E0` and `sigma_t` are drawn **independently**. If both were scaled by the same
  field, `kappa0 = sigma_t/E0` would stay uniform and the randomisation would not
  move the onset of damage at all.
- The ultimate strain is specified as the *ratio* `kappa_c_ratio = kappa_c/kappa0`
  rather than an absolute `kappa_c`. Once the strength is random, `kappa0` has an
  unbounded upper tail and a fixed `kappa_c` would be exceeded by the strongest
  elements, breaking the model's requirement `kappa_c > kappa0`.
- The Weibull tails are truncated at the 1st/99th percentiles
  (`WEIBULL_CLIP_QUANTILES`). Over ~40000 coal elements the extreme order
  statistic otherwise produces single elements at a small fraction of the mean
  strength, which fail immediately and dominate the peak damage without
  representing anything physical.

Because of that scatter, the peak damage `sup(D)` is a poor stopping criterion —
one weak element reaches `D = 1` very early. The run instead stops on the **mean
damage in the coal** (`--stop-coal-damage`, default 0.3), i.e. when the seam has
failed as a whole. This is what the `stop` callback of `runLoadingDissipation`
is for.

## Porosity

`SmoothDamageModel.getPorosity` gives

    phi = phi0 + b(D) * trace(eps) + crack_porosity * D

with a damage-degraded Biot coefficient `b(D)`. Note the competition in
compression: `trace(eps) < 0`, so the poroelastic term *reduces* porosity
(compaction) while the dilatant `crack_porosity * D` term increases it. Whether
the damage band ends up conductive or resistive depends on which term wins, i.e.
on `crack_porosity` relative to `biot`. The default `crack_porosity = 0.05`
makes the band net dilatant, the expected behaviour for brittle failure in
compression, but it is a parameter worth varying deliberately.

## Options and output

```
runCompression.py [--results DIR] [--max-steps N] [--stop-coal-damage D]
                  [--seed S] [--uniform] [--displacement] [--no-material-silo]
```

`--uniform` gives the deterministic reference run (no random heterogeneity),
`--seed` selects the realisation.

In the results directory (default `run/`):

| file | contents |
| --- | --- |
| `material.silo` | initial properties incl. the Weibull fields and layer tags |
| `history.csv` | axial strain/stress, damage and porosity statistics per step |
| `step<NNN>.silo` | `D`, `u`, `porosity`, `sigma_zz` for VisIt |

`history.csv` reports global and coal-only averages side by side, since the
seam carries almost all of the deformation. Compression counts positive.

## Notes for the ERT stage

Three things do not carry over from the other fingal examples:

- A lab specimen has **no padding region** — its boundary is physical. All six
  faces are separately tagged (`Top`, `Bottom`, `SideX0`, `SideXL`, `SideY0`,
  `SideYL`) so the config can choose; `core_tags` is the whole specimen.
- Every face is **insulating**: there is no far-field boundary at which to set
  the potential to zero, unlike a half-space survey. The potential has to be
  pinned some other way, otherwise the Neumann problem is singular.
- With the mesh in mm, a conductivity in S/m would give resistances off by a
  factor of 1000. Either work in S/mm or scale the result.
